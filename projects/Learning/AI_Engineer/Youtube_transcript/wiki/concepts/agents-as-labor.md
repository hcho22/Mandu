# Agents as Labor

The business model where an agent sells a completed job rather than a tool - targeting the labor market instead of software seats - and the solo-founder practice of using agents to do the work of a whole team.

## From the sources

[[agents-are-the-new-saas]] is the anchor thesis:

- SaaS sells software; agent SaaS sells work. The buyer offloads a task, and the market is labor (human capital), which is larger than software.
- Pick a workflow with a paycheck attached; build the Minimal Useful Agent (ladder: draft -> triage -> coordinate -> act) rather than a fully autonomous employee; the product wrapper (logs, approvals, a test surface) is what makes it SaaS.
- Sell pilots like labor first (run it manually with AI for three customers in one niche), then productize the repeated pattern; move toward outcome-based pricing once value is understood.

[[one-person-company-with-ai-agents]] lives the thesis from the founder side:

- A solo founder runs engineering, marketing, sales, and ops with agents to "do the work of ten people"; the shift is to invest heavily up front in systems, docs, and processes (build the machine first), then augment with more agents or an agent-augmented human.
- Building features is now almost trivial; the real skill is go-to-market and adoption.

[[building-5-ai-products-solo]] shows the portfolio version:

- One founder runs five products in parallel ("a garden"), shipping fast; monetization discipline: keep a paid tier for anything with hosting costs, and avoid ultra-cheap plans that create disproportionate support load.

## Synthesis

Two faces of the same shift: agents let one person deliver what used to take a team, so you can either **sell that labor as a product** (agent SaaS) or **run your own company on it** (the solo founder). The unifying reframe from [[agents-are-the-new-saas]] is that the sellable unit becomes a finished job, not a tool, which changes pricing (toward outcomes) and go-to-market (sell the disappearance of a painful workflow). The builders converge on "build the machine first" - the counterintuitive claim that in the agent era you invest *more* up front in systems, documentation, and processes, the opposite of old lean-MVP advice, because those systems are what the agents run on ([[agentic-coding-workflow]], [[agent-skills]], [[scheduled-agent-autonomy]]). This is the most business-adjacent concept in the wiki, but it stays in scope because the product *is* an agent and the moat is agent quality: the "product wrapper" that makes an agent trustworthy is the [[evals-for-agents]] control room plus [[agent-permissions-and-safety]] approvals, and outcome pricing is only defensible if you can prove outcomes. It also names the honest bottleneck - the constraint is no longer building, it is distribution and adoption - which is directly relevant to hcho's own product bets, where the same "validate the buyer, then build" discipline applies.
