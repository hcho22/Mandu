# 50 Things I'd Do If I Were Starting Over in My 20s or 30s

*A tech creator's high-level advice for building an irreplaceable career in the age of agentic AI — filmed on a working recharge in Korea.*

**Format:** ~50 numbered tips grouped into 5 themes.
**Speaker:** Career-switcher (PM → Staff Software Engineer at Meta) who now teaches Claude Code / Codex courses and runs a YouTube channel + newsletter.
**Framing:** Explicitly *not* a doom-and-gloom video — it's about how to adapt and put your best foot forward as AI reshapes junior tech work.

---

## Overview

The speaker reflects on graduating into the 2010 post-housing-crisis recession and argues that today's AI-driven shift "feels different — almost existential," especially for people early in their careers. Rather than despair, he offers ~50 concrete moves he'd make today. The core thesis: **code is becoming cheap, so the durable value shifts to judgment, taste, domain ownership, and mastery of agentic tools.** Junior-level tasks are automating first, but he argues that if AI could truly replace *all* engineering, almost no white-collar job would be safe — so the smartest response is to learn the tools deeply rather than flee them.

> 💬 **OPINION — Speaker's overall stance:** This is a personal, opinion-heavy talk — "advice I'd give my younger self." He repeatedly stresses these are *his* takes, not universal truths, and that viewers should do their own research and form their own feel for the tools.

**How to read the callouts:** 💡 **TIP** — actionable move · 🛠️ **TOOLS** — named tools/products · 📏 **RULE** — general principle · 💬 **OPINION** — the speaker's personal view · ⚠️ **CAUTION** — pitfall to avoid

## Part 1 · Mindset — Don't Panic, Read Reality Correctly

*Before any tactics: recalibrate how you interpret the flood of AI news and social-media noise.*

### 1. Your feed is not the marketplace

Negative sentiment sells; rage-bait and doom posts get outsized engagement. The "100 applications, zero offers" posts mostly come from people still angry and still in the trenches — a biased sample, not the whole market.

> 💡 **TIP:** Do your own deep dives on the actual data: real unemployment numbers, which jobs aren't being posted, which companies are and aren't hiring. Don't let anything be spoon-fed to you.

> 📏 **RULE:** Negative posts reliably get more attention — a documented pattern, not just opinion. Treat your feed as a distorted signal.

### 2. Never ask a barber for a haircut

Every AI company has an incentive to hype progress (AGI, self-iterating AI, "I don't code anymore") to keep investment and FOMO flowing. There's real truth in the hype, but built-in bias too.

> 💬 **OPINION:** He genuinely believes learning agentic coding tools like **Claude Code** or **Codex** is "probably the best thing you can do for your career," and that AI is here to stay — *but* take predictions with a grain of salt and form your own view.

### 3. Demos lie; production tells the truth

An **MIT** study found ~**95%** of GenAI adoption efforts at major (Fortune 500) companies had so far failed to show meaningful progress. He notes the study is a bit old (pre-**Opus 4.5**, November) so numbers may have shifted, but the lesson stands: ignore one-shot demo brags from PMs and "grifters."

> 🛠️ **TOOLS — cited as "survived in production":** **Codex** and **Claude Code** — held up under wide, real usage. He views most other AI tooling as still "just demos," regardless of valuation.

> 💬 **OPINION:** Most people — even at bleeding-edge firms — still aren't sure how to leverage these tools well. Even flagship features (he cites "Claude Code Design") remain in preview with kinks unsolved.

### 4. Don't judge your insides by others' outsides

LinkedIn / Threads / X show everyone's highlight reel — a shiny new Claude Code "skill" that changes everything. Reality: those skills are often flaky, took heavy iteration, and don't work 100% of the time.

> 💬 **OPINION:** GitHub stars no longer equal great engineering — they've become marketing. Whoever games the hype cycle for stars gets crowned "best," and he doesn't buy it. Focus on your own growth.

### 5. You are not behind

He became a software engineer at **28** via a bootcamp (right when he got married), ~10 years ago, and felt hopelessly behind next to new grads. His message: if you're in your 20s or 30s, you can start now and still progress fast — even with AI dominant.

> ⚠️ **CAUTION / counter-argument:** If AI could truly erase 100% of engineering jobs, almost no job under the sun would be safe. So choosing to learn the AI tools is a rational hedge, not a trap.

### 6. Fear is a crowded trade

Borrowing from investing (Warren Buffett: be greedy when others are fearful): people are fleeing CS because they fear automation. He doesn't see full replacement happening with the current architecture — AI is "a super genius and very stupid at the same time," needing constant guidance, good validation, and taste it doesn't have.

> 💬 **OPINION:** CS is still extremely valuable and now is the best time to learn it well — foundations, data structures & algorithms, system design — because that makes you a better *agentic* engineer later.

## Part 2 · The New Type of Engineer — What Actually Holds Value

*If code is getting cheap, value migrates to judgment, taste, and tool mastery. This is the meat of the video.*

### 7. Own the problem, not the code

Code is losing value; deep domain knowledge and expert problem-understanding stay valuable. When code becomes cheap, high-quality *ideas* become more valuable. He cites the classic lopsided team (≈1 PM, 1 designer, 12 engineers) as evidence that selecting the right ideas was always the scarce resource.

> 📏 **RULE (via Steve Jobs):** Knowing which ideas *not* to pursue is as important as knowing which to build. Go deep into one domain and own that problem space to root level.

### 8. Taste is the moat

Fully AI-generated work (writing, images, video) is still usually obvious. The most human part is *taste* — and encoding your taste so the machine can reproduce your judgment is central to agentic engineering.

> 💡 **TIP:** Actively study the greats and the work that inspires you — in code, UX, design, "how things should look and feel" — then slowly build your own taste. That's the real moat.

### 9. Agentic engineering is the new expertise

He calls this one of the most important new skills. Pillars he lists: **context engineering**, **agentic validations**, **agentic tooling** (building tools for the agent), and **compound engineering**. As models improve, the "meta work" of getting agents to do more on your behalf becomes the real leverage.

> 🛠️ **TOOLS — people referenced:** **Andrej Karpathy** frames tool-use skill as a genuine skill gap. **Peter Steinberger** joked he "does agentic engineering during the day and vibe-codes around 2 a.m."

> 💬 **OPINION:** He teaches this in his own course; regards it as essential work for engineers, PMs, and designers alike — "don't sleep on it."

### 10. Ship things that last

A reputation for durable, high-quality work goes a long way when AI makes it cheap to spray out unchecked code.

> 💬 **OPINION (contested topic):** He still reviews code before landing to production; notes AI code review is improving and this stance may change. Either way: **you own your code.**

> 📏 **RULE (via Buffett):** Reputation takes ~20 years to build and 5 minutes to lose. Don't torch it by shipping garbage and blaming the AI.

### 11. Follow the cost of being wrong

The more expensive it is to be wrong, the higher the quality bar AI must clear before it can fully take that work — so those domains stay human-led longer.

> 💡 **TIP:** Target problems where being wrong is costly (why medicine, law, and heavily-regulated fields resist deep AI penetration). Avoid building your identity around cheap-to-automate work like landing pages and front-end scaffolding.

### 12. Aim to be top 10% (ideally top 1%)

He flags this as controversial. Because companies can be "disconnected from reality" and lay off for opaque reasons, the safest position is being undeniably good.

> 💬 **OPINION:** He admires leaders like **Jensen Huang** (NVIDIA), who said he'd "rather torture you to greatness than fire you." But you can't count on that — aim for top 10%, and even that isn't a guarantee.

### 13. Learn the layers below

AI coding tools are a new abstraction layer stacked on top of existing ones (languages, React, Vue, Kotlin, Jetpack Compose). All abstractions are "inherently leaky" — foundations can break or degrade.

> ⚠️ **CAUTION:** Without base understanding of what's under the hood, you'll lose control at production time — security issues and more. This "happens all the time" to people who skip fundamentals.

> 💡 **TIP:** Default to curiosity: whenever you don't understand how something works, dig one layer deeper. You don't need every library, but chase the foundations.

### 14. Read more code

Counter to the "stop reading, AI reviews better" crowd. He personally spends more time reading than writing code — but reads *selectively*.

> 💡 **TIP — what to read vs. skip:**
> - **Read the hard stuff:** difficult patterns and system designs you wouldn't invent yourself. Example: to build agentic tools, learn the **ReAct loop** — essentially a while-loop where the agent reasons → decides on tool use → acts → repeats until done. Study open-source agents (e.g., "OpenCode") to see how it's really built.
> - **Skip / let AI handle:** trivial, easily-verified code (centering a div, boilerplate React components) that unit tests or screenshot tests can validate.

### 15. Ignore titles, focus on shipping

Early in his career, shipping a lot helped him move up fast. The best ideas win regardless of title.

> 💡 **TIP:** If you're new, don't let your position hold you back — use AI and every tool available to build and test your ideas.

### 16. Learn where the AI fails

Enough hands-on use builds a "second sense" for what AI can and can't do — itself a skill. You start instantly mapping a request to the right pipeline (data extraction → context gathering → AI systems that automate the work).

> 📏 **RULE:** LLMs only ever hallucinate; the useful outputs are just "useful hallucinations." Build intuition for the boundary.

> 💡 **TIP:** Keep a log. Deliberately push systems to their limits and record where they succeed and fail.

### 17. Build a T-shaped portfolio

(He made a full earlier video on this.) Broad breadth lets you know what's possible with AI and jump between domains quickly to orchestrate multiple agentic tools at a high level. But you also need at least one deep, foundational area of real depth.

> 💬 **OPINION:** This T-shape — wide breadth + one deep stack — is what he believes recruiters are currently looking for.

### 18. Master the AI tools

Possibly his most important tip: know **Claude Code** or **Codex** like the back of your hand — how it loads memory, manages context, plus slash commands, skills, agents, multiple sub-agents, agent teams, "teleporting" instances, remote control, and scheduling loops.

> 🛠️ **TOOLS — broader tooling to master:** Beyond coding: video generation, image generation (which model for which purpose), voice models, and local LLMs via **Ollama**. He believes mastering these tools alone can get you hired.

> 📏 **RULE (analogy):** Like a professional woodworker who knows every tool cold — these agentic tools are "the tools of the future," so learn them to that standard.

### 19. The world's best tutor is an AI

He learns almost anything with AI — Blender, Adobe, unfamiliar software, deep dives into codebases, even TPUs vs. GPUs.

> 📏 **RULE:** The real prerequisite is developing a genuine *desire to learn*; the tutor is now essentially free and always available.

## Part 3 · Career Strategy — How to Get Ahead

*Concrete moves for standing out, building trust, and navigating companies.*

### 20. Push it to prod (ship end-to-end)

(Nod to his newsletter, **Push to Prod** on Substack.) The failure mode is "chasing the red dress" — endless new shiny side projects, nothing finished.

> ⚠️ **CAUTION:** You learn the most from taking one thing zero-to-shipped and *maintaining* it. Senior people "have the scars to prove it"; those who never shipped and maintained for 1–3 years lack the depth to handle what inevitably comes up.

### 21. Communication is the largest leverage

Articulating ideas well is the killer skill most people lack. Unless you're a once-in-a-generation engineer like **John Carmack**, communication is how you stand out.

> 💬 **OPINION:** He started YouTube partly to get better at speaking; treating it as a trainable skill means he's never afraid to present. In an AI-noisy world, this is a soft skill AI can't easily replace.

> ⚠️ **CAUTION:** Don't sound like you're reading an AI script — it will hurt your reputation.

### 22. Become worth vouching for

He's "optimized for people" — found strong leadership, worked hard to earn their trust, and became someone happy to be vouched for (still in touch with his first SWE manager).

> 📏 **RULE:** The industry is smaller than it looks — people cluster and move together (he cites talent flowing into Gemini, and ex-Meta people across OpenAI/labs). Reputation and referrals compound over years; referrals are the best way to get hired.

### 23. Do what feels like play

Orchestrating multiple Claude Code instances across panes feels like playing **StarCraft** to him — genuinely fun. Not everyone will feel that, and that's fine.

> 📏 **RULE (via Steve Jobs):** Long-term success comes from finding something you're truly passionate about and sticking with it — because passion is what carries you through the hard, frustrating stretches when others quit.

### 24. Follow the money (into boring industries)

Top labs / FAANG may be out of reach without elite internships. But heavily-leveraged AI usage is often happening in "boring" industries — finance, farming, bespoke niches.

> 💡 **TIP:** Seek uncrowded places with high AI demand and few people looking into it — you'll find them by following the money.

### 25. Take risks before you can't

He quit his job at **28** for a bootcamp with little money — a big risk that paid off. Now, with two kids and a good salary, the same risks would be far costlier.

> 📏 **RULE:** Startups and learning-optimized bets get harder as you age. People rarely regret failures late in life — they regret the risks they didn't take. Take them while young.

### 26. Optimize for slope, not salary

Early on, maximize the *slope* of your learning, not the paycheck. He chose a job paying ~$20K less for a better learning opportunity and is glad he did — it accelerated him, and $20K is now trivial next to his year-end bonuses (multiples of that).

> 💡 **TIP:** A steep enough learning curve makes up salary gaps within a few years. Don't optimize for a little more money here or there.

### 27. Own the ugly (but important) work

Shiny, prestige work is crowded and its credit flows to leads and seniors. Unglamorous-but-critical work (a migration, some core piece of tech) often goes unowned.

> ⚠️ **CAUTION — the caveat:** The ugly work must be genuinely *important*. Don't confuse it with ugly-*and*-worthless work. Also be vocal about the value you're delivering.

> 💡 **TIP:** Owning thankless-but-critical work consistently, and doing it well, moves you up quickly.

### 28. Be the bridge between your company and AI

There's a clear skill gap — even principals, architects, and senior engineers mostly aren't sure what's actually possible unless they've logged serious hours in Claude Code / Codex. He was able to lead his company's AI-transformation work because he had.

> 💬 **OPINION:** Because it's all so new, even a junior or newer engineer can lead efforts a principal would normally own — principals are stuck in meetings and often weren't coding. The people investing ~10 hrs/day in these tools end up better positioned.

> 💡 **TIP:** Be the person who can answer: what integrations are doable, at what scale, and at what cost. That positions you extremely well.

### 29. Network before you need it

"Opportunities are attached to people, not job boards." Best long-term network: genuine relationships with colleagues over time — big tech companies build it almost automatically as people cycle in and out.

> 💡 **TIP:** No big-company network? Build one via social media and content. He met many tech creators through his own content — approached genuinely, with collaboration as a bonus, not the goal. Build it *before* you need it, when it's easiest and most sincere.

### 30. Win the interview

Interviewing is a learnable (if unpleasant) skill. Has AI changed it? Yes and no.

> 📏 **RULE — what's still tested:** **LeetCode-style coding** and manual coding assignments persist (still the most direct proof you can code); companies are ~a year behind on changing this. **System design** matters even more with rising abstraction — expect it graded harder.

> 💬 **OPINION — what's coming:** Within 1–2 years he "could almost guarantee" a new interview type around AI/agentic coding, since tool use is now core to the job. Some AI-assisted interviews already exist; everyone's still figuring it out.

> 💡 **TIP:** Still win it the classic way: data structures & algorithms, system design, behavioral practice, then brush up on each company's specific process. Best advice remains: join the best tech company you can, or a very well-funded, bleeding-edge AI startup.

### 31. Pick the manager, not the logo

Your manager largely determines how enjoyable and successful your job is — they fight for you in rooms you're not in, protect your time, and steer you to the best projects.

> 💡 **TIP — vetting a manager:** Look up the chain: your manager, their manager, and theirs — how strong are those connections? Check how long they've worked together, and how many strong ICs want to work with that manager. Getting onto a great manager's "bench" pays dividends for career growth *and* sanity.

### 32. Find great mentors

His mentors saved him years — across technical deep dives (e.g., performance), career navigation, and even YouTube.

> 💬 **OPINION:** There's a limit to what AI can teach: with too many choices it may "glaze" you (over-agree / flatter). A human mentor course-corrects and helps you learn taste and "what good is" faster.

### 33. Teach to learn

You don't truly know something until you can teach it to a beginner. He's trained hundreds of engineers on Claude Code / Codex / agentic engineering — a side course plus internal sessions with hundreds of attendees — which he earned by learning deeply and advocating hard (starting with internal videos).

> 📏 **RULE — the teaching flywheel:** Teaching creates more chances to teach; wanting to teach each new feature makes you learn it deeply; students' questions expose gaps in your understanding. (Caveat: not all great engineers are great teachers.)

### 34. Pick a direction and stick with it

Too many started-never-finished projects means mistaking motion for progress.

> ⚠️ **CAUTION — the mountain trap:** Jumping to a new "mountain" every time you hit a hard peak feels productive (early problems are familiar) but teaches you nothing new — you keep re-hitting the same early stage. Pick one direction, go as deep as you can. This also builds the depth of the T-shape (see #17).

### 35. Build / learn in public

It takes courage to share unfinished work, but it builds tough skin, pushes you to finish, and makes you highly discoverable for networking — even jobs. He cites open-source projects that "go crazy" and mint staple developers in the new AI ecosystem.

> 💬 **OPINION:** Building and sharing in public is extremely valuable early in a career — self-improvement plus easy, organic networking.

## Part 4 · Money & Health — Build Your Foundation

*A "tiny cage" of low expenses and savings buys the freedom to take the risks above. Plus: not personalized financial advice.*

### 36. Audit your expenses

Most people carry subscriptions they don't need. Audit everything, keep the core (learning, food, essentials), save the rest.

> 💡 **TIP:** Get your house in order — you'll be surprised what's slipping through the cracks.

### 37. The first $100K is the hardest

Saying: first **$100K** is very hard, the next **$1M** also hard, the next **$5M** feels impossible — but milestones make future money easier.

> 📏 **RULE:** $100K saved gives real safety and wiggle room, and studies suggest those who reach it are far more likely to reach $1M later.

### 38. Set it and forget it (index investing)

Pick an index (he mentions the **S&P 500**), invest monthly, make it a fixed expense, and let compounding work. Saving for a rainy day matters given an uncertain future.

> ⚠️ **CAUTION — disclaimer:** He explicitly and repeatedly states this is **not investment advice**.

### 39. Spend on your AI tools

The one thing not to skimp on. Max plans for Claude Code / Codex run about **$200**, and heavy use can hit rate limits fast. He pays personally (and gets work tokens) because it's *learning*.

> 💡 **TIP:** Get so good you can debug, understand the layers below, and know skills, plugins, and context management. Use them now — the current $200/usage ratio likely won't last forever.

### 40. Rest is non-negotiable

Ironically, AI has him working *more* — many builders are like this (he has a video on "being addicted to Claude Code").

> 📏 **RULE:** It's a marathon, not a sprint. There are times to push and times to rest; without habits of working out, relaxing, and resting, you won't last.

### 41. Build escape hatches / multiple income streams

His streams: day job, YouTube, newsletters, his course — and he's always hunting new ones. The motive is the uncertain future and the "K-shaped economy," where token-holders may hold disproportionate power.

> 💡 **TIP:** Build multiple escape hatches, save, and invest so inflation doesn't erode it — a hedge in case AI does take over more than expected.

### 42. Train like your life depends on it

Desk work is punishing — nearing 40 he has wrist and lower-back pain and wishes he'd stretched, used a standing desk, and done yoga earlier. Now health is a high priority.

> 📏 **RULE:** Marathon, not a sprint (again): don't let your body become the limiting factor on your growth and success.

## Part 5 · Staying Ahead — Bet on Yourself

*Being early is a durable edge; the final push is backing yourself.*

### 43. Be an early adopter

Early adoption lets you "live in the future" and spot trends. When **Opus 4.5** launched (November, with a double-rate holiday window) he switched all workflows immediately — "something clicked." By the time industry usage of Claude Code exploded (spiking late January into February), he'd been optimizing skills for months and was ready to *teach*, which created new scope and leadership for him.

> 💡 **TIP:** Be early not just on coding tools but on voice models, image models, and AI search (he names **Perplexity**). Early investigation gets you ahead of the curve.

### 44. Follow the leaders

To stay early, keep an ear to the ground. People he follows: **Boris Cherny**, **Andrej Karpathy**, **Peter Steinberger**, **Dario Amodei**, **Sam Altman**, and **Elon Musk** (he stresses he ignores politics and just wants the signal).

> 💬 **OPINION:** Take "ridiculous" leader claims seriously — e.g., Jensen Huang's line that a $500K engineer who doesn't spend $250K worth of tokens should concern him. Whether it lands depends on believing tokens are a unit of work (he has a separate video on tokens/loops). He built his own AI news source with Claude Code to stay current.

### 45. Bet on yourself

If you've built financial freedom, back yourself — quit and switch careers, go for the big job, or start your own thing. He bet on himself at every step, going from PM to Staff Software Engineer at Meta.

> 💬 **OPINION:** Now is a great time to bet on yourself while many are sleeping on efficient tool use. Get ahead and become an expert and you'll go far — in a tech career or on your own.

### 46. Ship while slightly embarrassed

He dislikes "MVP" — minimally viable often isn't ready. Aim a bit above: something you feel decent about that covers many edge cases. But if your V1 doesn't slightly embarrass you, you probably waited too long.

> ⚠️ **CAUTION:** Perfectionists talk about shipping but never ship. Obsessing over V1 details means you never release — and never get the feedback and ideas that only arrive after shipping.

### 47. Luck = preparation meets opportunity

One of his favorite sayings (he thinks from the movie *Serendipity*). Opportunities are everywhere, but you can only seize them if you've been training, preparing, learning, and building.

> 📏 **RULE:** In a booming AI era opportunities are abundant (easy funding, easy startup interviews) — but preparation is what lets you actually capitalize.

### 48. Your first job is not your final job

A job is just a job. Losing one, or taking a non-ideal first one, isn't the end — you'll have many. Take the best job you can now and keep moving; seize new opportunities as they come.

> ⚠️ **CAUTION:** Don't over-weight the first job (young people especially do). The real risk is complacency — keep evolving and growing rather than settling into one role.

### 49. Build for the model of the future

If you build agentic applications, build for the model that will exist *next*, not today's. Each model generation unlocks the previously-impossible (Opus 4.5 + Claude Code's maturity was such a moment).

> 💬 **OPINION — via Boris Cherny:** He heard this at a talk with Boris: Claude Code was built assuming future models would catch up to its features. Building only for current models is easy to copy and limits your thinking to what's possible today.

### 50. Don't let AI atrophy your skills

Going deep on AI tooling tempts you to stop coding manually. Manual coding may matter less now, but keep studying system design and fundamentals (space-time complexity, reading code) — atrophy sets in faster than you think.
